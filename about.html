Personal Story

About Me

Health, I learned early, is never just clinical. Growing up in Syria, I watched illness follow the lines of poverty, displacement, and exclusion — and I noticed that the people most affected by a health problem were often the last to have accurate information about it.

That observation has followed me through every project I've taken on since. Whether I'm studying why university students can't put down their phones, adapting first-aid materials for someone who's never seen a diagram, or coordinating a health session for parents and teachers trying to rebuild a sense of normalcy — I keep coming back to the same question: what do people actually know about their health, and what's stopping that knowledge from reaching them?

I'm a medical student at Damascus University, but most of what shaped how I think about medicine happened outside the lecture hall, in research I pursued on my own initiative, in community rooms with Caritas, and in first-aid sessions with participants who taught me more about accessibility than any textbook did. I also spend a good part of my time trying to make sure other students don't have to learn research methodology the hard, self-taught way I did — which is why I built MedRAN.

I'm currently applying to graduate programs in public health and medical education, hoping to turn years of hands-on, self-directed work into the kind of rigorous training that lets me do it at scale.
